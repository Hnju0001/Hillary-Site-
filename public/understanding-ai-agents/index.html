A Field Guide, Not a Sales Pitch

Understanding AI Agents:
what they actually do, in plain English

Software that acts on your behalf, one claim, ticket, or transaction at a time — and the honest story of where that helps, where it doesn't, and how to tell the difference.

⏱ ~40 minute read 📎 No technical background required 🗃 Two running case studies throughout
Section 00

A broken headlamp — why this matters

Before we define anything, here's the experience that makes the rest of this guide worth reading.

Cedar & Pine Outfitters is a mid-size outdoor gear company. They sell tents, hiking boots, headlamps — the kind of gear people buy once, use hard, and expect to last. Their support inbox handles about 6,000 messages a week.

Cedar & Pine is a fictional, composite example. Its numbers are illustrative — chosen to be realistic, not copied from any single real company.

TICKET #48213RESOLVED
"My headlamp won't turn on. I'd like a replacement."
Customer: M. Alvarez · Order placed 11 days ago · Product: Trail Pro 600 headlamp · Channel: email

Without an agent, this ticket sits in a queue. A support rep picks it up hours later, looks up the order, checks the warranty policy, confirms Alvarez qualifies, processes a replacement, and writes a reply. Time from receipt to resolution: roughly 14 hours, almost all of it waiting in line.

With an agent, the same ticket is read automatically. The agent pulls up order #48213, checks that the purchase was 11 days ago (well within the 30-day warranty), confirms the Trail Pro 600 is in stock, issues a replacement, and drafts a reply — all before a person would have opened it. Time: roughly 4 minutes.

The key distinction, before we go further

A chatbot could have told Alvarez what the warranty policy says. It could not have looked up the specific order, confirmed eligibility, or issued the replacement. An agent doesn't just answer. It acts — executing delegated procedural work within strict human-designed boundaries. That single difference — conversation versus action — is the subject of this entire guide.

Watch the same request handled three ways

Automation

Fixed rule engine

"If message contains 'won't turn on' AND category = electronics, send templated reply #14: 'Please try charging for 2 hours.'"

No lookup. No judgment. Same reply regardless of order history or warranty status.

Chatbot

Conversational, but hands-free

Interprets the question flexibly and can explain the warranty policy clearly in plain language.

But it cannot check the actual order, confirm eligibility, or issue anything. It ends with "please contact support."

AI Agent

Evaluates, then acts

Reads the message, calls a tool to pull up the real order, calls another to check policy, decides Alvarez qualifies, and calls a third to actually issue the replacement.

The difference isn't the conversation. It's that something changed in Cedar & Pine's systems as a direct result.

It's tempting to look at "14 hours" versus "4 minutes" and conclude agents are simply better. Hold that thought. That particular ticket — a defective item, clearly within policy, with no ambiguity — is close to the best case for an agent. Later in this guide, we'll follow a ticket from the same inbox where the agent makes things worse, not better. The honest picture requires both.

Governing Layer · Human Authority

Human authority is not a sixth stage at the end of a line — it is the governing framework surrounding all five levels, retaining final judgment, policy oversight, and escalation control.

Cluster A — The building blocks (nothing here is specific to AI)

Software

Any set of instructions a computer follows. A calculator app, a spreadsheet, and a website are all software. Software does exactly what it was written to do — no more, no less.

Automation

Software written specifically to carry out a task without a person clicking through each step. A nightly script that backs up a database is automation. It follows fixed rules — it doesn't decide anything.

Artificial Intelligence (AI)

Software that is trained on examples rather than told exact rules, so it can recognize patterns in new situations it hasn't seen before. Instead of "if the total is over $500, flag it," an AI system learns what a suspicious claim tends to look like.

Workflow

The full sequence of steps needed to get something done, from start to finish — filing a claim, onboarding an employee, shipping an order. A workflow can involve people, software, and AI agents.

Cluster B — How a language model "reads" and "writes"

Large Language Model (LLM)

A specific kind of AI, trained on enormous amounts of text, whose core skill is predicting what text should come next. That one skill — done extremely well — turns out to be powerful enough to answer questions, summarize documents, and draft replies. It is not the same thing as an AI agent; it's usually one component inside one.

Tokens

The small chunks — often pieces of words — that a language model actually reads and writes. "Kitchen ceiling" might become two or three tokens. The model doesn't see letters or sentences the way you do; it sees a long string of these chunks and predicts the next one.

Context Window

The maximum number of tokens a model can "hold in view" at one time — its instructions, the conversation so far, any documents it's been given. Think of it as desk space: even a big desk eventually runs out of room.

Prompt

The instructions and information you give a model before it responds. In agents, prompts are usually written by whoever built the system, not typed fresh each time.

One honest caveat, early

Language models sometimes produce confident, fluent, completely wrong answers. This is called hallucination. It isn't a rare glitch — it's a natural side effect of how these models work: they're predicting plausible next words, not consulting a database of facts. We'll come back to this repeatedly.

Cluster C — How a system actually reaches out and does something

API

Short for "Application Programming Interface" — a defined way for one piece of software to ask another to do something, without a human clicking buttons. When Cedar & Pine's agent checks an order, an API is how that request travels.

Tool

In agent terminology, a "tool" is a specific capability made available to an AI system through an API — "look up this order," "check the weather on this date," "send this email." An agent can only use the tools it's been explicitly given.

Retrieval

Fetching a specific, current fact from an outside source — a database, a document, a live record — instead of relying on whatever the model absorbed during training. Retrieval is one of the main defenses against hallucination.

Structured Output

Getting a model to answer in a fixed, predictable format — a filled-in form with named fields — rather than a paragraph. "Claim number: 88213, Damage type: water" is structured. A structured answer can be read directly by other software.

Cluster D — How an agent is actually built and coordinated

13 · The term this whole guide is about

AI Agent: a system that combines a language model with a defined set of tools, and is allowed to take more than one step — deciding what to do, doing it, checking the result, and deciding what to do next — in pursuit of a goal, within boundaries a person designed in advance. The key shift: an agent doesn't just answer you. It acts, across multiple steps, usually touching real systems along the way.

Loop

The repeating cycle inside an agent's work: think about what to do, do it, look at what happened, decide what to do next. One ticket might take an agent through this loop five or six times.

Harness

The surrounding code that manages the loop responsibly: how many times it's allowed to repeat, what happens if a tool fails, when to give up and hand off to a person. If the loop is the engine, the harness is the brakes and seatbelt.

Memory

Short-term memory is what happened earlier in this same task. Longer-term memory is information retrieved from storage between separate tasks. Neither kind means the model "learned" anything permanently.

Orchestration

The logic that decides, across a whole workflow, which agent runs when — what happens first, what runs in parallel, what triggers a handoff to a person. If individual agents are specialists, orchestration is the schedule that makes them work together.

Multi-Agent System

Several specialized agents, each with a narrow job and its own small set of tools, coordinated by an orchestration layer. This is what we'll see inside Meridian Mutual — not one all-powerful agent, but several modest ones working in sequence.

MCP (Model Context Protocol)

A shared, open standard that lets AI systems connect to tools in one consistent way rather than needing custom wiring for every connection. Engineers describe it as "a USB-C port for AI" — one connector instead of a drawer of incompatible cables.

Nineteen terms, four clusters, one direction of travel: from plain software, to a model that can read and write, to a system that can reach out and act, to several such systems working together. Keep that direction in mind — it's the spine of everything that follows.

💡 If you only remember one thing about agents

An AI agent isn't defined by conversational fluency — it's defined by delegated action. It takes a goal, uses tools to execute multi-step workflows, and operates strictly within boundaries designed and governed by humans.

From headlamp to hurricane

Cedar & Pine's headlamp ticket showed what a single agent can do for a single customer. The same principles — tools, loops, confidence, escalation — operate at much larger scales. The next section introduces an organization where that scale becomes the whole point.

Section 02

Meet Meridian Mutual — life before agents

Every concept in this guide will now be tested against one recurring situation. Here's the situation.

It's the end of August. A category-3 hurricane has just moved through a coastal region where Meridian Mutual insures roughly 40,000 homes. Within 48 hours, 5,000 claims arrive. Meridian's normal team can process about 800 claims a week. This is more than a month's workload arriving in two days.

Meridian Mutual is a fictional, composite example. Its numbers are illustrative — chosen to be realistic, not copied from any single real insurer's disclosed figures.

Fig. 1 — The manual claims process, before any AI7 stages, almost entirely human
1 · Intake

A clerk keys each claim into the system by hand.

2 · File Assembled

Documents and photos are attached to a case file.

3 · Queue

The file waits its turn. During a surge, days disappear here.

4 · Adjuster Review

A person checks the policy, reviews photos, estimates damage.

5 · Fraud Referral

If something feels off, the adjuster flags it.

6 · Manager Sign-off

A manager approves the payout before money moves.

7 · Payment

Funds are released to the policyholder.

Nothing about this process is unreasonable in ordinary times. The problem is volume: every stage requires a person's attention, and during the surge, the queue at Stage 3 stretches from hours to three to six weeks.

Speed

Customers whose homes are damaged wait weeks for an answer, at the exact moment they need money for repairs.

Strain

Adjusters work sustained overtime, reviewing far more files per day than the role was designed for.

Consistency

Tired, rushed reviewers are more likely to miss a real fraud pattern — or misjudge a legitimate one.

This is the situation the rest of this guide keeps returning to. It's a specific, recognizable bottleneck: too many structured, repetitive decisions, arriving faster than the people who make them can keep up.

Section 03

Foundations — tasks, decisions, and trust

Before we talk about AI at all, we need four ideas that have nothing to do with AI. They're about how work is structured.

3.1 — Answering is not the same as acting

This is the single cleanest way to tell a chatbot from an agent, and we saw it in Cedar & Pine's headlamp ticket.

A chatbot responds. An agent does something.
A ChatbotAn AI Agent
What it producesInformation — an answer to a questionA completed step — something changes in the world
Meridian example"Your claim status is: pending review."Verifies the policy, checks weather records, scores fraud risk, calculates an estimate, and routes the file — then reports what it did.
Cedar & Pine example"Our return policy covers items within 30 days."Looks up the actual order, confirms the headlamp was purchased 11 days ago, verifies stock, issues a replacement, and drafts a reply to Alvarez.
Who initiates each stepYou do — one question, one answer, repeatThe system does, until it's done or stuck

3.2 — Not all decisions deserve equal trust

A well-built system doesn't just produce a decision — it produces a decision plus a sense of how sure it is. That confidence level determines what happens next:

Key Autonomous Flagged for review Escalated Human decides

A note of realism: these confidence percentages are not automatically reliable. A model's own certainty scores tend to be poorly calibrated out of the box — they must be tested against real outcomes and adjusted before they can be trusted for routing decisions in production.

The idea underneath all of this

A good agent isn't one that's always right. It's one that knows when it might be wrong, and routes exactly those cases to a person — while handling the confident, low-risk majority on its own.

Section 04

What "AI" actually means

Before we can talk about what an agent does, we need to be honest about what's happening inside the "AI" part.

4.1 — Rules versus patterns

Ordinary software follows rules a person wrote: if the claim total is over $10,000, require manager approval. AI works differently. Instead of being told the rule, it's shown thousands of past examples and learns, statistically, what pattern of features tends to go with which label. It doesn't "understand" fraud the way an experienced adjuster does. It recognizes that claims with certain combinations of traits resemble past fraudulent claims. That's a real and useful capability. It is not the same thing as judgment.

4.2 — LLMs: one very capable tool, not the whole toolbox

A large language model's one core skill is predicting the next chunk of text. Trained on enough text, that single skill becomes remarkably general — it can draft messages, summarize documents, or extract specific details from free-form writing. These are pattern-based systems capable of producing reasoning-like behaviour — not conscious thinkers, but far more capable than simple autocomplete.

Here's roughly what the model sees when a customer types: "Water came through the kitchen ceiling during the storm and ruined the cabinets."

Water came through the kitchen ceiling during the storm and ruined the cabinets.

Highlighted chips are the fragments that matter most for extracting "damage location" and "damage type" into a structured form.

Hallucination, made concrete

Suppose the claim form says the incident happened on July 5, but is a little smudged. A language model reading it might confidently extract "July 15." It isn't lying — it's producing the most statistically plausible reading, and in this case, the plausible reading is wrong. This is why every date, dollar figure, and policy number the model reads gets checked against the actual source record — a discipline called retrieval.

4.3 — So what is "intelligence," here, really?

Precise, narrow pattern recognition, applied consistently, at a scale no group of humans could sustain — not judgment, values, or understanding. That's a genuinely useful thing to have. It is also a fundamentally different thing than what the word "intelligent" suggests to most people.

Section 05

The anatomy of an agent

An agent is not one clever algorithm. It's several plain engineering pieces, assembled deliberately.

5.1 — Tools and APIs: what an agent is actually allowed to touch

An agent can only do what it's been given a tool for. Meridian's engineers deliberately restricted each specialized agent to a short, specific list. This is a security decision as much as a design one: an agent that can only read the weather database can't accidentally authorize a payment.

💡 If you only remember one thing about tools

Tools are an agent's hands. An agent cannot touch any system or data source it hasn't been explicitly given an API tool for — limiting tool access is our primary way to keep agents secure.

5.4 — Memory: two very different things

Short-term (within one claim)

The Assessment agent doesn't re-ask for the policy number the Intake agent already extracted — it's carried forward as working context. This disappears once the claim is finished.

Longer-term (across many claims)

The Fraud Detection agent can retrieve this customer's claim history from storage, going back years. This is retrieval against a database, not the model "remembering."

Neither kind means the system is learning in the sense a person learns from experience. Models are not continuously retrained from every interaction. What does improve over time is the surrounding system: engineers refine prompts, tune confidence thresholds, update retrieval databases, and tighten harness rules based on observed performance. This is genuine, meaningful improvement — but it is engineered, not experienced.

💡 If you only remember one thing about memory

Agents don't "remember" or learn permanently from experience. They carry working notes in short-term context and look up records in long-term databases — fetching verified facts to prevent hallucination.

5.5 — The harness: the car around the engine

Left unsupervised, a loop could repeat forever, call a failing tool over and over, or spiral. The harness is the code wrapped around the loop that prevents that: a cap on repeats, a timeout if a tool doesn't respond, a rule that says "after three failed attempts, stop and escalate." None of this is intelligence — it's engineering discipline.

5.6 — Orchestration: the schedule that makes specialists behave like a team

Meridian's orchestration logic, in plain language

1. Run Intake. If the form is unreadable, stop and ask a person to key it in.
2. Run Coverage Verification. If the policy doesn't cover this, deny with a reason — stop here.
3. Run Event Confirmation and Fraud Detection at the same time (neither depends on the other).
4. If Fraud Detection's risk score is high, route to a human investigator regardless.
5. Otherwise, run the Assessment agent, then the Audit agent to prepare a clean summary.
6. A human adjuster reviews the summary and authorizes payment.

💡 If you only remember one thing about orchestration

Complex workflows don't use one giant agent. They use an orchestration manager that coordinates multiple small, narrow specialist agents — passing clean structured data between them.

What actually changed — and what didn't

The adjuster team didn't get smaller, and no agent gained the authority to approve a payout. What changed is where human attention gets spent: instead of people spending the same twenty minutes of data entry on each of 5,000 claims, the team spends its hours on the roughly 1,500 files that genuinely need judgment. Redistribution of human attention, not removal of it.

💡 If you only remember one thing about authority

Agents handle repetitive data collection and initial evaluation, but financial, legal, and ethical decisions remain anchored to human authority. Automation redistributes human attention to where it matters most.

Section 08

Where agents excel — and where they don't

Two questions predict almost everything: how structured is the decision, and how much volume is there?

Volume ↑
Structure of the decision →
High volume · Low structure

The danger zone. Frequent, but genuinely ambiguous — e.g., autonomously moderating social media posts. Pattern-matching at scale amplifies mistakes on the cases where judgment matters most.

High volume · High structure

The sweet spot. Frequent and well-defined — Meridian's claims pipeline, Cedar & Pine's warranty lookups. Clear steps, abundant past examples. This is where agents earn their cost.

Low volume · Low structure

Not worth automating. Rare and ambiguous — a sensitive apology letter to a loyal customer like Okafor. An AI assistant can help draft it; a dedicated agent is wasted effort.

Low volume · High structure

Traditional software wins. Well-defined but rare — a report generated twice a year. A simple script does this perfectly well.

The section this guide most wants you to read

When an agent is the wrong tool

Most tasks, most of the time, are better served by something simpler. Here's how to tell.

9.1 — The ticket that goes the other way

Remember Cedar & Pine? The headlamp ticket was close to the best case for an agent. Here's a different ticket from the same inbox:

TICKET #52108AUTO-RESOLVED (poorly)
Automated reply, sent 40 seconds after receipt:
"Thank you for contacting Cedar & Pine. Per our returns policy (section 3.1), items must be returned within 30 days of delivery. Your order falls outside this window and is not eligible for a refund. We appreciate your business."

T. Okafor has ordered from Cedar & Pine for six years. A jacket bought 42 days ago — 12 days past the standard 30-day return window — has a zipper that's failed. Okafor's email opens by mentioning six years of loyalty and closes with "I will never order from you again."

Procedurally, the agent's reply is not wrong. The order genuinely is outside the window. But Okafor posts about it publicly, disputes the charge, and stops ordering. A single accurate policy citation turned a $110 jacket into a much larger loss.

What actually went wrong

Not a bug. Not a hallucination. The agent did exactly what it was built to do. What it lacked was authority and judgment — the ability to recognize that a rule applied literally can still be the wrong move. A person handling this ticket would have options the agent structurally doesn't: acknowledge the frustration, note that six years of loyalty is worth more than one jacket, and offer a replacement as a goodwill exception.

9.2 — When conventional software is simply sufficient

Example: calculating sales tax. Exact arithmetic — multiply the price by a known percentage. A few lines of code will get this right every single time. An agent introduces a real chance of getting basic math wrong.

9.3 — When simple automation is preferable

Example: syncing warehouse inventory to a website every night at 2 a.m. Repetitive, scheduled, zero judgment. A scheduled script does this reliably and cheaply.

Five sentences that predict a struggling agent project
  • "We don't have a defined process — the AI will figure it out."
    Agents execute a process well. They don't design a good one for you.
  • "We have a little data — the model will pick up the rest."
    Sparse examples produce unreliable pattern-matching, not intuition.
  • "This decision is subjective, but the AI seems smart enough."
    Values-based judgment needs human authority — that's not a capability gap AI closes.
  • "We'll sort out oversight after we launch."
    Escalation rules need to exist before real cases flow through, not after.
  • "We want full automation — humans just slow things down."
    The goal is redistributing human attention — not removing the one thing that catches mistakes.
The actual opportunity, stated honestly

The realistic case for agents isn't "replace the support team." It's "remove the repetitive 60% of the queue so the humans who remain spend their time on the 40% that actually needs a human" — which, done well, is a better job, not a smaller one.

Section 10

Common misconceptions

Each persists for a real, specific reason — usually a word carrying more meaning in everyday speech than it does in an engineering diagram.

Myth

Once deployed, an AI agent operates independently — humans are optional.

Reality

Every reliable deployment keeps a person at the highest-stakes point. "Autonomous" describes a bounded zone of confident, low-risk decisions — not the removal of oversight.

Why this persists

The word "autonomous" carries decades of science-fiction baggage. In engineering, it just means "doesn't need approval for this specific step."

Myth

An AI agent understands situations the way a person does.

Reality

It's recognizing statistical patterns. Fluent, confident output is not evidence of understanding — it's evidence of a model trained to produce fluent, confident output.

Why this persists

Humans are wired to read fluent language as a sign of a mind. That instinct is usually right about other humans and misleading about language models.

Myth

Giving an agent more tools and more data makes it more capable.

Reality

Agents juggling many loosely related tools tend to perform worse — every additional tool is another chance to pick the wrong one. Meridian's agents each carry two or three tools, not twelve.

Why this persists

"More resources should mean more capability" is true almost everywhere else. Tool selection is the specific place this intuition breaks down.

Myth

The more claims an agent processes, the smarter it automatically gets.

Reality

Learning from experience requires a deliberately built feedback loop. Without that infrastructure, an agent's decision rules stay exactly as they were on day one.

Why this persists

People naturally improve with repetition. Whether a system learns from its own outputs is a deliberate design decision — not a built-in feature.

Myth

Because it's a computer program, an AI agent's decisions are objective and unbiased.

Reality

An agent learns whatever pattern is present in its training examples — including any historical bias baked into who got flagged, denied, or approved in the past. At scale, it can repeat bias far more consistently than any one person would.

Why this persists

"Computer" and "math" both carry a cultural association with neutrality that doesn't automatically transfer to systems trained on human-generated data.

Myth

Deploying agents means the people who did that job are no longer needed.

Reality

At Meridian, the same adjuster team remains — spending its time on the 30% of claims that genuinely need judgment. At Cedar & Pine, support staff spend more time on the Okafor tickets and less on the Alvarez tickets. It's fair to say some roles do shrink, and that transition deserves real planning rather than denial.

Why this persists

It's a reasonable fear grounded in real automation history — even when it's not what the data on most current deployments shows.

Section 11

Is an agent right for your problem?

Five questions, asked in order. A "no" at any point is a genuine, useful answer — it means something simpler is the right call, not that you've failed to find the AI angle.

Question 1
Is the task repetitive and high-volume?
No → Use traditional software or do it by hand. No scale to justify an agent.
Yes → Continue.
Question 2
Is it highly structured — clear steps, clear decision points?
No → Too open-ended for pattern-matching. This needs human judgment, possibly with an AI assistant helping draft options.
Yes → Continue.
Question 3
Do you have abundant, representative past examples to learn from?
No → Not yet. Gather clean historical data first — an agent trained on too little data will make unreliable calls.
Yes → Continue.
Question 4
Can a person review the consequential decisions before they're final?
No → Redesign the workflow so someone can. An agent with no human check on high-stakes outcomes is the configuration this guide has repeatedly flagged as risky.
Yes → Continue.
Question 5
Is success clearly measurable?
No → Define your metrics first. You can't tune confidence thresholds for a goal you can't measure.
Yes → You likely have a good candidate — the shape of Meridian's claims problem, or Cedar & Pine's warranty lookups.

Section 12

The road ahead

Deliberately split in two: what the data shows, and where informed judgment takes over. Mid-2026 is early enough that the honest answer to many "what happens next" questions is genuinely "we don't fully know yet."

Evidence What's already measurably true, as of mid-2026

  • Agents are being embedded into existing software fast. Industry analysts project that a significant and growing share of newly shipped enterprise applications will include at least one AI agent by end of 2026 — up from less than 5% in 2025. The exact figures vary depending on how "AI agent" is defined and who is counting, but the direction is consistent across sources.
  • Returns are genuine — and so are failures. Organizations report a median payback period around five months. At the same time, a substantial share of agent initiatives — estimates above 40% — will be scaled back or cancelled, usually over unclear goals or weak governance, not a lack of capability.
  • Multi-agent coordination and shared connection standards are maturing. A meaningful share of production deployments now coordinate three or more agents, and MCP has moved from a new idea to widely supported in about a year.
  • Adoption is real but uneven across industries. Insurance and banking lead; healthcare and government lag behind, consistent with the caution those sectors' stakes usually warrant.
A sobering counterweight

The same analysis that shows rapid adoption also shows a high failure rate for individual projects. The two facts aren't contradictory — they're the same story: a genuinely useful technology being deployed, in a meaningful fraction of cases, onto the wrong problems or without sufficient governance. The diagnostic in Section 11 exists specifically to keep a given project on the right side of that split.

Speculation Reasonable guesses, held loosely

  • Standardized connections may reduce lock-in. As MCP matures, switching the underlying model or vendor inside an agent system may become easier — though how much easier, and how fast, isn't settled.
  • Governance and disclosure norms will probably formalize further. Expect clearer expectations — and possibly regulation — around when an organization must disclose that a decision involved an AI agent.
  • Job roles will keep shifting more than disappearing — the pattern this guide has described — but which specific roles change shape depends on choices individual organizations haven't all made yet.

If you take one idea from this section: the honest current state is real adoption, real value, and a real failure rate — not a settled verdict in either direction. Treat any confident claim about where this technology will be in five years with the same healthy skepticism this guide has tried to model throughout.

Section 13

Frequently asked questions

Shorter, more direct answers to specific things people usually want to know once they've read this far.

Are AI agents conscious, or actually "thinking"?
No. They recognize learned patterns and generate plausible next steps. There's no inner experience, no intention, and no understanding — however fluent the output sounds.
How much does something like this cost?
It varies enormously. A narrow, pre-built agent for a common task can run from roughly $50 to a few hundred per month. A custom agent built around a company's own systems is a larger project — often tens of thousands in setup — but frequently paid back within months if the task it handles was previously a meaningful chunk of labor cost.
What happens when it gets something wrong?
In a well-scoped system: it escalates to a person, ideally before real harm is done, using triggers like the confidence thresholds in Section 03. In a poorly-scoped one, like Okafor's refund dispute: it confidently does the wrong thing, and a person finds out after the fact. The difference is almost entirely in how carefully the boundaries were designed.
Is it safe to let an agent access company data?
Scope it the way you'd scope any employee's access: least privilege first. Read-only access to lookup systems is lower-risk than write access to payment systems — exactly the read/write distinction shown in the tool access table in Section 05.
Can an agent override its own instructions?
A model can produce text that conflicts with its instructions — a known risk sometimes called prompt injection. But in a well-designed system, the agent's real-world impact is bounded by which tools it can access and what the harness allows, not by trusting the model to obey every time. The practical defense is constrained permissions, harness rules, and human governance — not hoping the model always follows directions.
How long does deployment take?
Longer than most vendor pitches suggest. The real timeline is dominated by process redesign, safe integration, and governance rules — not the AI component. Months, not days, is realistic for a genuine first deployment.
Do I need to tell customers they're dealing with an agent?
Practices are actively evolving. As a general posture: disclosing plainly tends to build trust, and increasingly it's becoming an expectation rather than a courtesy.
How do I know if a task I have in mind is actually a good fit?
Run it against the five-question diagnostic in Section 11. If it scores well on most of those questions, it's worth investigating. If it scores poorly — especially on "well-defined" or "data-driven" — a person, or simple automation, is probably the better tool.
If the agent made a mistake, is it broken?
A 95%-accurate agent handling a task humans get right 85% of the time is a genuine improvement, mistakes included. The right question isn't "did it ever fail" — it's "how does it fail, and how expensive is that failure?"

Appendix · Section 14

Glossary & Reference Appendix

Every term used in this guide, in one quick-reference alphabetical index.

Quick Jump: [ A ] [ C ] [ E ] [ H ] [ L ] [ M ] [ O ] [ P ] [ R ] [ S ] [ T ] [ W ]
A
AI Agent
A system combining a language model with tools, allowed to take multiple steps toward a goal within boundaries set by its designers. Acts, rather than only answering.
Artificial Intelligence (AI)
Software trained on examples to recognize patterns, rather than following explicit rules.
API
Application Programming Interface — a defined way for one piece of software to request something from another.
Automation
Software built to carry out a fixed, rule-based task without human involvement at each step. Does not make judgment calls.
C
Chatbot
Software that holds a conversation, typically answering from a script or knowledge base. Can talk; cannot, on its own, take action in other systems.
Confidence Threshold
The certainty level above which a system acts on its own, versus flags for human review.
Context Window
The maximum text (measured in tokens) a language model can consider at one time.
E
Escalation
An agent recognizing a task is beyond its confidence, authority, or scope, and routing it to a person — ideally with full context.
H
Hallucination
When a language model produces a fluent, confident, and factually wrong answer — a natural side effect of predicting plausible text rather than consulting verified facts.
Harness
The code surrounding an agent's loop that manages retries, timeouts, logging, and escalation rules. The "vehicle" around the LLM "engine."
L
Large Language Model (LLM)
An AI system trained on text to predict what comes next — often the reasoning component inside an agent.
Loop
The repeating think, act, observe, decide cycle an agent runs through while working a task.
M
MCP (Model Context Protocol)
An open standard, introduced in late 2024, for connecting AI systems to tools in one consistent way, avoiding custom wiring for each connection.
Memory
Information an agent can refer to — either within one task (short-term) or retrieved from storage (longer-term). Distinct from a model "learning" permanently.
Multi-Agent System
Several specialized agents coordinated by an orchestration layer to complete a workflow no single agent handles alone.
O
Orchestration
The logic deciding which agent runs when across a workflow, including handoffs to people.
P
Prompt
The text given to a language model describing what's wanted. The entire channel through which intent reaches the model.
R
Retrieval
Fetching a fact from a real, current source and placing it in the context window, rather than relying on trained-in patterns. The primary defense against hallucination.
S
Software
Any set of instructions a computer follows. The baseline everything else in this guide is a variation of.
Structured Output
A model's answer in a fixed, predictable format — named fields rather than free prose — so other software can act on it directly.
T
Token
A small chunk of text, often a word fragment, that a language model reads and writes. What usage and limits are measured in.
Tool
A specific capability made available to an agent through an API — the mechanism by which an agent reaches outside itself to check data or take action.
W
Workflow
A sequence of steps that turns a starting situation into a finished outcome. What automation, assistants, agents, and people are all, ultimately, different ways of running.

This guide is an educational reference, not a product pitch. Cedar & Pine Outfitters and Meridian Mutual Insurance are composite, illustrative examples, not real companies. Figures throughout are proportioned to reflect patterns reported across real deployments, not audited results from any single organization. Where this guide discusses contested questions — particularly around jobs — it aims to represent more than one reasonable view rather than argue for one.
